## A. Errors

1. **Thresholds, not values (L187): "Fed into a 5 V ATmega328P input, it lands above the 3.0 V threshold, so it *will* be read as 1"**
   - Category: hardware fact / reasoning. Severity: **error**.
   - Problem: Espressif's datasheet guarantees only V_OH ≥ 0.8 × VDD, which is 2.64 V at 3.3 V. That is *below* the ATmega328P's guaranteed V_IH of 0.6 × V_CC = 3.0 V at 5 V. Reasoning "from the datasheet's numbers", which the next sentence asks for, says the opposite: a 1 is **not guaranteed**. The "few tenths of a volt" margin applies only to a lightly loaded pin that is close to 3.3 V, which is a typical value, not a guarantee. The margin also shrinks further when the Uno's V_CC is above 5 V (USB allows up to 5.25 V, which gives V_IH = 3.15 V). The Sources "Inference" note labels only "may fail in a noisy environment" as inference, so the false "will" is presented as fact.
   - Evidence: ESP32 Series Datasheet v5.3, Table 5-3 "DC Characteristics (3.3 V, 25 °C)": V_OH min 0.8 × VDD; I_OH 40 mA "VDD = 3.3 V, VOH >= 2.64 V" (https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32_datasheet_en.html). ATmega328P V_IH = 0.6 V_CC (see item 2).
   - Fix: "Its guaranteed HIGH (0.8 × 3.3 V = 2.64 V) is below the 3.0 V an ATmega328P input needs to guarantee a 1, so the datasheets promise nothing. A lightly loaded ESP32 pin is usually near 3.3 V and will usually be read as 1, but with only about 0.3 V to spare…". Use a level shifter as the engineering conclusion. Label the "usually" part as inference.

2. **Experiment 01.01-3 (L280), Answer (L358), Sources (L366): "V_OH ≥ 2.2 V while the output carries 10 mA"**
   - Category: datasheet value / arithmetic. Severity: **error**.
   - Problem: the ATmega328P datasheet gives V_OH min **2.3 V** at I_OH = −10 mA, V_CC = 3 V (T_A = 85 °C). The HIGH margin at 3 V is therefore 2.3 − 1.8 = **0.5 V**, not 0.4 V. The other 3 V values (V_IL 0.9 V, V_IH 1.8 V, V_OL 0.6 V, LOW margin 0.3 V) are correct. The conclusion that margins shrink still holds (0.3/0.5 V against 0.6/1.2 V).
   - Evidence: Atmel-42735B-ATmega328/P_Datasheet_Complete-11/2016, §32.2 Table 32-2 "Common DC characteristics": V_OH "IOH = -20mA, VCC = 5V, 85°C: 4.2"; "IOH = -10mA, VCC = 3V, 85°C: 2.3" (copy at https://fab.cba.mit.edu/classes/863.17/EECS/people/akaspar/project/atmega328p-datasheet.pdf). The same values (4.2 / 2.3) appear in the translation of the ATmega48A/PA/88A/PA/168A/PA/328/P datasheet at https://www-user.tu-chemnitz.de/~heha/hsn/ATmegaX8.chm/30.htm. The 2.2 V figure appears only on an unnamed Microchip page whose V_IL row (0.2 V_CC for 2.7–5.5 V) shows it belongs to a different device.
   - Fix: change 2.2 V to 2.3 V in all three places, and change "HIGH margin: 2.2 − 1.8 = 0.4 V" to "2.3 − 1.8 = 0.5 V".

3. **Table 01.01-1 (L40): "A register or SRAM cell | A pair of transistors locked in one of two voltage states"**
   - Category: hardware fact / internal consistency. Severity: **error**.
   - Problem: an SRAM cell is two cross-coupled *inverters* (four transistors) plus two access transistors, six in all. A register bit is a flip-flop with far more transistors. "A pair of transistors" is wrong. It also contradicts §01.04, which uses "an SRAM cell 6" transistors (book/s0104/01.04.md, Experiment 01.04-5).
   - Fix: "A pair of cross-coupled inverters (feedback) locked in one of two voltage states".

4. **Hands-on (L286): "a breadboard and three jumper wires"**
   - Category: experiment. Severity: **error**.
   - Problem: the wiring at L288 needs four connections from the breadboard to the Uno: 5V, GND, wiper to A0, and wiper to D2.
   - Fix: "four jumper wires".

5. **Journey (L54) and History box (L147): "some built machines on the answer 'ten'" / ENIAC "held each digit … in a ring of ten circuits" / von Neumann "argued instead for binary: each element would need only two states, making the elements simpler and the whole machine more reliable"**
   - Category: history / conceptual. Severity: **error**.
   - Problem: the journey asks how many voltage levels *one wire* should carry. ENIAC is offered as the machine built on "ten", but ENIAC never used ten voltage levels. Its decade ring counters were ten *two-state* trigger circuits (flip-flops), so each digit was coded one-hot, with one active out of ten. ENIAC's elements were already binary. The difference was the number system, not the number of physical states per element. The paraphrase of von Neumann therefore misrepresents him. In the First Draft, §5.1 notes that the trigger circuits have "two equilibria" and concludes "it is natural to use a system of arithmetic in which the digits are also two valued"; §5.2 argues that binary arithmetic is logically simpler ("does away with the decimal multiplication table"). "More reliable" is not his argument. As written, a reader will conclude that ENIAC used ten voltage levels on a wire. That is exactly the misconception the section exists to remove.
   - Evidence: First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, §§5.1–5.2 (https://comp.anu.edu.au/courses/comp2310-2020/Papers/1945/von%20Neumann/The%20First%20Draft%20Report%20on.pdf). The report is dated 30 June 1945 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Draft_of_a_Report_on_the_EDVAC).
   - Fix: say explicitly that decimal electronic machines (ENIAC, IBM 650) still built every element from two-state circuits and merely *grouped* them into decimal digits (ten per digit in ENIAC, seven in the 650's bi-quinary code). Binary won because grouping two-state elements in binary is more economical (about 3.3 elements per decimal digit instead of 10) and the arithmetic is simpler. Restate von Neumann's argument accordingly. If the journey keeps "some built machines on the answer 'ten'", give a real multi-state example such as the ten-position counter wheels of electromechanical machines (Harvard Mark I), or Setun's three states, or drop the claim.

6. **Front matter (L5): `builds: §00.01, §00.02`; PDF p.1 "Builds on §00.01, §00.02"**
   - Category: cross-reference / TOC. Severity: **error** (mismatch with the approved TOC).
   - Problem: toc.json, ref 01.01, has `"builds": "§00.01"`. The section does use §00.02 material (Listing 00.02-1, the gcc flags), so either the TOC or the front matter must change. They must agree.
   - Fix: change the front matter to `§00.01`, or get the TOC entry amended to "§00.01, §00.02".

   - Problem: the project uses only the Uno. Nothing in it uses the ESP32.

## B. Likely errors

8. **Self-check 4 answer (L324): "a movement of only about 0.28 V, smaller than the noise usually produces"**
   - Category: arithmetic / statistics. Severity: **likely error**.
   - Problem: for σ = 0.4 V, P(|n| > 0.278 V) = 2Q(0.694) = **0.487**. The median |n| is 0.6745σ = 0.27 V. Noise exceeds 0.28 V in about half the readings, not "usually". That is also why the error rate is 44 %, not more.
   - Evidence: `python3` with erfc: P(|n|>0.2778) = 0.4874.
   - Fix: "…a movement of only about 0.28 V, which the noise exceeds in about half of all readings, crosses into a neighboring level."

9. **Table 01.01-1 (L42): "Flash memory (an SSD, the Uno's program memory) | Charge trapped on an insulated 'floating gate'"**
   - Category: hardware fact. Severity: **likely error**.
   - Problem: most current SSDs use 3D NAND with a *charge-trap* layer (silicon nitride), not a floating gate. Samsung, Micron, SK Hynix and Kioxia use charge trap. The Uno's flash is floating-gate, so that part is correct.
   - Evidence: TechTarget: "Samsung, Micron, SK Hynix, and Kioxia … have turned to charge trap technologies for most of their flash storage"; "some manufacturers have stuck with floating gate cells" (https://www.techtarget.com/searchstorage/tip/Charge-trap-technology-advantages-for-3D-NAND-flash-drives).
   - Fix: "Charge trapped in an insulated layer (a 'floating gate' or a charge-trap layer)".

10. **History box (L147): "The first large electronic computer, ENIAC"**
    - Category: history. Severity: **likely error**.
    - Problem: Colossus (1943–44; the Mark 2 had about 2,400 valves) was a large electronic machine that predates ENIAC. ENIAC is normally described as the first *general-purpose, programmable* electronic digital computer.
    - Fix: "The first general-purpose electronic computer, ENIAC, …". (Based on standard histories; not fetched.)

11. **In orbit box (L237) and Figure 01.01-3: "A small strike leaves the voltage within the 'read as 1' band, and the cell's own feedback restores it … A large one pushes it across the threshold"**
    - Category: physics. Severity: **likely error** (imprecise mechanism).
    - Problem: the figure draws the bands from the ATmega328P *I/O-pin* input guarantees (1.5 V / 3.0 V). Whether a storage cell flips depends on whether the struck node crosses the switching point of the cell's own cross-coupled inverters, which is about V_CC/2 and lies inside the "uncertain" band. A dip into the "uncertain" band that stays above the switching point still recovers. "The threshold" is undefined, since the figure shows a band, not a line. (§01.02 introduces the inverter switching point later.)
    - Fix: in the text, "…as long as it stays on the 1 side of the cell's switching point (roughly half the supply), the feedback restores it". In the figure, draw a single dashed "switching point" line near 2.5 V, or label the bands as illustrative and state that they are not the I/O-pin limits.

12. **Journey (L48): "Voltage is a difference in electrical potential energy between two points: the energy that each unit of electric charge would gain or lose"**
    - Category: physics precision. Severity: **likely error** (the reader asked for strict correction).
    - Problem: a "difference in electrical potential energy" has units of joules. Voltage is a difference in electric *potential*, which is potential energy *per unit charge* (J/C). The colon clause corrects it, but the head clause is wrong. Terms introduced (L245) gets it right.
    - Fix: "Voltage is a difference in electric potential between two points: the energy that each unit of charge (each coulomb) would gain or lose…".

13. **Journey (L50): "Those three abilities correspond to *set*, *hold and move*, and *read*."**
    - Category: reasoning. Severity: **likely error**.
    - Problem: the second ability listed is "it travels along a wire", which is moving, not holding. A voltage on a wire does not hold itself; holding needs feedback or stored charge (§01.04). The mapping blurs the set/hold/read principle just stated at L29–31.
    - Fix: "…correspond to *set*, *move* and *read*; *holding* a voltage needs extra circuitry, which §01.04 builds."

14. **Experiment 01.01-2 (L278) "the *Precisely speaking* box" and Sources (L366) "quoted in the *Precisely speaking* box"**
    - Category: internal cross-reference. Severity: **likely error** (ambiguous).
    - Problem: there are three Precisely speaking boxes (L138, L179, L222).
    - Fix: name them, e.g. "the Precisely speaking box after Figure 01.01-1" and "the Precisely speaking box after the noise-margin discussion".

## C. Improvements: technical precision

15. **Precisely speaking (L180): "the highest voltage at which a pin is *ensured* to be read as low"**: the italics suggest a quotation. The datasheet notes say "guaranteed" (Atmel-42735B Table 32-2 notes 1–2; Microchip online docs for ATmega328PB: "'Max.' means the highest value where the pin is guaranteed to be read as low", https://onlinedocs.microchip.com/oxy/GUID-0EC909F9-8FB7-46B2-BF4B-05290662B5C3-en-US-12.1.1/GUID-6021D919-A79A-408F-970A-7BE478329E6E.html). Use "guaranteed", or remove the emphasis.
16. **Output's promise (L173): "Those limits hold even while the pin supplies 20 mA"**: for the LOW limit the pin *sinks* current. Fix: "…while the pin sinks (LOW) or sources (HIGH) 20 mA".
17. **L177 "a gap that no correctly working output ever produces" and Myth (L184) "2.5 V … a voltage that a correctly working output never produces"**: every output passes through this region during each transition, and an overloaded output can sit in it. Add "except briefly while switching", which also prepares for sampling and clocking later.
18. **Myth (L184): "(indeed up to half a volt above it)"**: V_CC + 0.5 V is the edge of the absolute-maximum rating, where the input clamp diodes start to conduct. It is not a normal operating point. Add "although you should never design to rely on that".
19. **Table 01.01-3 (L206–207): "the range of `int` on the ATmega328P" / "on your laptop"**: 65 536 and 4 294 967 296 are the *number of values*, not the range, since `int` runs from −32 768 to 32 767. Fix: "the number of values an `int` can hold on…".
20. **Word (L220) and Self-check 9 answer (L334)**: the ATmega328P datasheet itself uses "word" for 16 bits (flash is organized in 16-bit words), and Intel x86 terminology uses WORD = 16 bits, DWORD = 32, QWORD = 64. The reader will meet both usages. Add one sentence warning that "word" is also used as a fixed name for 16 bits in AVR and x86 documentation. Qualify "A word on such a machine holds eight bytes" accordingly.
21. **History (L147): "Decimal machines lingered through the 1950s … two states became universal"**: decimal machines were sold in large numbers into the 1960s (IBM 1401 and 1620, both 1959). Every one of them used two-state elements, so "universal" holds for *elements*, not for number representation. The next paragraph (multi-level flash) already contradicts "universal". Fix: "two-state elements became universal; binary number representation became the norm."
22. **The question (L16): "Why does every computer you will ever program store information using only *two* states"**: the reader's own SSD stores 4 to 16 levels per cell (L150). Change to "almost always … two states", or let L150 explicitly answer this as the exception.
23. **Figure 01.01-1 text (L134): "With ten, every curve spills across the lines on both sides"**: the 0 V and 5 V curves have a decision line on one side only. Also, "never meet the dashed decision line" is true only visually, since normal curves have infinite tails (the Precisely speaking box says so). Fix: "…every inner curve spills across the lines on both sides", and "…stay visibly clear of…". The figure has no vertical-axis label; consider "how often (relative)".
24. **Figure 01.01-2 (fig2.png)**: the green dotted guide at 3.0 V and the blue dotted guide at 0.9 V run through the right-axis tick labels "3 V" and "1 V", which partly obscures them. Stop the guides at the axis or move the labels.
25. **Figure 01.01-3 (fig3.png)**: the band labels use I/O-pin limits on an internal storage node (see item 11). The legend text is about 5 pt at the printed width, small but legible.
26. **In orbit (L237): "a proton trapped in the Earth's radiation belts, can pass straight through a chip, freeing electric charge along its path"** / **"Even on Earth, particles from cosmic rays occasionally flip bits"**: in most technologies trapped protons upset cells mainly *indirectly*, through nuclear reactions that release heavy recoils. At sea level the main culprits are secondary neutrons, through the same indirect mechanism, and alpha particles from the chip's own packaging. Add a clause; this matters for §35.01. (From standard SEE literature; not fetched.)
27. **Self-check 10 and L237: "Smaller transistors hold less charge, so modern chips need less charge to be upset"**: the critical-charge statement is right. However, the per-bit soft-error rate of SRAM has stayed roughly flat or fallen in recent nodes, because the collecting volume shrinks too. The rise is in errors per *chip and system*, because there are far more bits (Baumann, "Radiation-induced soft errors in advanced semiconductor technologies", IEEE TDMR 2005). "Tend to" softens it. Consider one sentence of nuance. (I could not fetch a source page to quote; see the "Could not verify" list.)
28. **Hands-on (L300–301): `float volts = raw * 5.0 / 1024.0;`**: the conversion assumes V_CC = 5.000 V. A USB-powered Uno is typically 4.7–5.1 V. Both the ADC reference (AVCC) and the input thresholds (0.6 V_CC) scale with V_CC, so the displayed "volts" can be off by several percent. The ADC's own absolute accuracy is ±2 LSB. Suggest measuring the 5V pin with a multimeter and substituting it, or also reporting `raw / 1024.0` as a fraction of V_CC, which is the quantity the datasheet's 0.3/0.6 × V_CC actually refers to.
29. **Hands-on safety (L288)**: add one caution. If the wiper is wired to 5V or GND by mistake and an outer pin goes to the other rail, turning the knob to that end stop shorts the Uno's 5 V supply through almost zero resistance. Check that the middle pin goes only to A0 and pin 2.

If it is a few tenths of a volt, "slightly" undersells it. Give the datasheet's typical figure, or say "by a measurable amount".

Chapter 18 covers `static` functions and §18.06 random numbers, but only the LCG, not xorshift. ## What I checked and found correct

- **Listing 01.01-1** compiles cleanly with `gcc-14 (14.2.0) -std=c23 -Wall -Wextra -pedantic`, with no diagnostics. Its output matches the book's output block **byte for byte**. With `sigma` changed to 0.2 and to 0.8 on line 25, the outputs also match the two blocks in the Answers byte for byte. The output was also unchanged with `-O2 -mfma -ffp-contract=fast` (gnu23), so the "does not depend on the compiler" claim held in my tests.
- **Line references**: line 6 (seed), lines 8–21 (`uniform`/`noise`), line 25 (`sigma`), 34 (send), 35 (voltage plus noise), 36 (receiver), 37–42 (clamp), 43–45 (count) are all correct. Every code line is ≤ 78 characters (maximum exactly 78 in Listing 1, 72 in Listing 2).
- **Noise model**: the Irwin–Hall sum minus 6 has unit variance, and |noise| < 6σ = 2.4 V, so zero two-level errors is exact for σ = 0.4 and 0.2 and not for 0.8 (6σ = 4.8 V).
- **Normal-distribution figures**: two levels Q(6.25) = 2.05 × 10⁻¹⁰ ("about 2 in 10¹⁰" ✓). Four levels 1.5·Q(2.083) = 2.79 % ("2.8 %" ✓). Ten levels 1.8·Q(0.694) = 43.9 % (simulation 44.4 %). Spacings 5.000/1.667/0.556 V ✓. 5/9 = 0.556 ✓.
- **Listing 01.01-2** compiles with `avr-g++ 7.3 -mmcu=atmega328p -Os -std=gnu++11` using a minimal mock of `Serial`/`analogRead`/`digitalRead`/`delay`. Run in simavr 1.6, it prints in the expected format (e.g. `2.60 V  ->  1`; raw 1023 shows as `5.00 V` because of rounding). The formula Vin = ADC·Vref/1024 matches the datasheet. About 5 readings per second is right: the roughly 15 characters per line fit in the TX buffer, so `delay(200)` dominates. The wiring is electrically safe (0.5 mA through the 10 kΩ potentiometer; both pins are high-impedance inputs).
