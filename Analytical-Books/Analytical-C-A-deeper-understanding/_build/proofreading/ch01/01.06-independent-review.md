## 1. Errors

### E1. Line 435 (orbit box): the AGC's memory was *larger* than the Uno's, not smaller. Category C. Severity: error
**Problem.** "every instruction had to earn its place in a memory smaller than the flash of your Uno."
**Evidence.** The text itself says 36,864 words of fixed memory. AGC words are 15 data bits plus a parity bit (Ken Shirriff, "Inside the Apollo Guidance Computer's core memory", righto.com, 2019: "each word was 15 bits plus a parity bit"; "36,864 words of ROM in core rope memory"; "2048 words of RAM"). That gives 36,864 × 15 = 552,960 bits = 69,120 bytes of program memory, against the Uno's 32,768 bytes of flash. The erasable memory is also larger: 2,048 × 15 bits = 3,840 bytes, against 2,048 bytes of SRAM.
**Fix.** "…had to earn its place in a program memory of about 69 KB of data bits, roughly twice the flash of your Uno, and an erasable memory of under 4 KB." Or drop the comparison.

### E2. Line 417: "computed x × 10 as (x + 4x)". Category C. Severity: error
**Problem.** x + 4x = 5x, not 10x. The rest of the sentence is correct, but this clause states a false equation.
**Fix.** "The compiler computed x × 10 as 2 × (x + 4x): `lea` forms x + x × 4 = 5x, and `add` then forms 5x + 5x = 10x…"

### E3. Line 416 (and Self-check 12 answer, line 618): "The same instruction can have several encodings" is illustrated with two *different* instructions. Category C. Severity: error
**Problem.** `lea (%rdi,%rsi,1)` and `lea (%rsi,%rdi,1)` are different instructions, with base and index swapped (SIB byte `0x37`: base rsi, index rdi… versus `0x3e`: base rsi, index rdi in the other order). They give the same result. They are not one instruction with two encodings. A reader who wants precision will take away the wrong idea.
**Evidence.** A genuine case does exist. `{store} add %rdi,%rax` assembles to `48 01 f8` and `{load} add %rdi,%rax` assembles to `48 03 c7`: the same instruction text, two encodings (checked with GNU as/objdump in /tmp/r0106/x86/enc.s).
**Fix.** Retitle the bullet "**The same operation can be written as different instructions.**" and change the answer at line 618 to match. Optionally add the `01`/`03` example to show the separate fact that one instruction can have two encodings, chosen by the assembler.

### E4. Line 415: the description of the padding leaves out one padding instruction. Category B/C. Severity: error
**Problem.** "The 11-byte and 2-byte instructions after each `ret` are never executed." After `add8`'s `ret` there are two padding instructions: the 11-byte `data16 cs nopw` (0x4–0xe) and a 1-byte `nop` (`90` at 0xf). After `add_big`'s `ret` there is one, the 2-byte `xchg %ax,%ax`. The 1-byte `90` is not mentioned anywhere. The list of lengths happens to include 1 byte, but only because of `c3`.
**Fix.** "The instructions after each `ret` (an 11-byte and a 1-byte `nop` after `add8`, a 2-byte one after `add_big`) are never executed…"

### E5. Line 590: "with one complication explained in §01.05" points to text §01.05 does not contain. Category A. Severity: error
**Problem.** The 32-bit-only access rule for ESP32 instruction RAM is not explained in §01.05's text. It appears only in §01.05's *Sources* list ("the 32-bit access rule for instruction memory", siblings/01.05.md line 769). Neither §01.05's Hands-on B (lines 673–708) nor its journey mentions it.
**Fix.** Explain it here in one sentence: "the ESP32's instruction RAM can be read through the data bus only as whole, aligned 32-bit words (Espressif, ESP-IDF *Memory Types*); a byte read raises a LoadStoreError exception." Delete "explained in §01.05".

### E6. Line 284 (Listing 01.06-2, line 3) contradicts line 305. Category B/C. Severity: error
**Problem.** The listing's comment says "Every line below becomes exactly one machine instruction." Line 305 says correctly that lines 5, 6 and 8 are directives, and labels and blank lines produce nothing either.
**Fix.** "; Every instruction line below becomes exactly one machine instruction."

### E7. Line 636 (Experiment 01.06-5 answer): "`add_big` became four instructions" and then lists five. Category B. Severity: error
**Evidence.** avr-gcc 7.3.0 `-Os` output: `subi r22,0x77`, `sbci r23,0x98`, `sbci r24,0xBA`, `sbci r25,0xDC`, `ret`.
**Fix.** "became four arithmetic instructions and a `ret`" or "became five instructions".

### E8. Lines 532/636: `-Wconversion` produces two warnings, but the answer explains only one. Category B. Severity: error
**Evidence.** `avr-gcc -mmcu=atmega328p -Os -Wall -Wconversion -c lengths.c` prints:
```
lengths.c:5:14: warning: conversion to 'uint8_t {aka unsigned char}' from 'int' may alter its value [-Wconversion]
lengths.c:9:14: warning: conversion to 'long int' from 'long long int' may alter its value [-Wconversion]
```
The `add8` warning comes first. A reader told to look for "the warning" will see it first and not be able to explain it.
**Fix.** In the question, say "what do the warnings say?". In the answer, add: "A second warning, on `add8`, appears because `a + b` is computed as `int` (integer promotion, §10.03) and narrowed back to `uint8_t`." Also cite §05.06 (integer constants: why `0x123456789` has type `long long` on the AVR) next to Chapter 10.

### E9. Lines 450 and 459: reading Figure 01.06-3 from the bottom does not give "this chapter in order". Category D/A. Severity: error
**Problem.** From the bottom up, the figure's boxes are: decoder and ALU (§01.02, §01.03), instruction register (§01.05), flash cells (§01.04), machine code, assembly, C. §01.05 comes before §01.04, and §01.01 is missing. The "six rungs" at line 459 are the six sections of the list, not the six boxes of the figure, which are different things.
**Fix.** Either reword ("Figure 01.06-3 touches every section of this chapter; in order, they are:") or redraw the ladder so that it reads §01.01 → §01.06 from the bottom. For example: voltages as bits (§01.01) → transistors and gates (§01.02) → adder/ALU (§01.03) → flash and registers (§01.04) → fetch–decode–execute (§01.05) → machine code / assembly / C (§01.06).

## 2. Likely errors

### L1. Line 86: the reason avr-gcc uses r18–r25 is stated as fact, and it is mostly not the reason. Category C. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** "That is why the AVR compiler … keeps working values in r18 to r25 wherever it can: those are registers that `LDI` … can reach. A choice about how to spend 16 bits … decides which registers your C variables live in today."
**Evidence.** The avr-gcc ABI (Microchip, *AVR GCC Compiler User's Guide*, §4.6 "Register Usage") makes r18–r27 and r30–r31 the call-used registers, which a function may use without saving, and r2–r17 and r28–r29 call-saved. Arguments go in r25 down to r8, which includes low registers. The values seen in §01.03 and §01.05 (r24, r22, r25, r23) are in those registers because they are argument and return registers. Reachability by `LDI` may well have influenced the ABI's design, but that is an inference, and the text presents it as the cause.
**Fix.** "Partly for this reason, the avr-gcc calling convention (§19.03) makes upper registers (r18–r27, r30–r31) the ones a function may use freely, and passes arguments and results in r8–r25, so the values you saw in §01.03 and §01.05 sit in r22–r25." Mark the causal link as inference.

### L2. Line 86 and Self-check 3 (line 600): "a program **must** load it into an upper register first and then copy it". Category C. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** That is not the only way. For the constant 1, `clr r5` followed by `inc r5` needs no upper register. A constant can also be loaded from memory (`lds r5, k`). The upper-register-plus-`mov` route is the general method, not the only one.
**Fix.** "…a program usually loads it into an upper register first and then copies it (for small values there are other tricks, such as `clr r5` then `inc r5`)."

### L3. Line 359: "to within less than a millionth of a second" mixes cycle-exactness with time accuracy. Category C. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** The cycle count is exact. The time in seconds is only as good as the clock. On the Uno R3 the ATmega328P is clocked by a 16 MHz ceramic resonator. Murata CSTCE16M0V53-type parts are specified at about ±0.15% to ±0.5% initial tolerance (Digi-Key/Farnell product listings), which is 0.75–2.5 ms per half-second. That is thousands of times larger than the 375 ns being claimed.
**Fix.** "…every 8,000,006 cycles exactly: 0.500000375 s if the clock were exactly 16 MHz. The Uno's ceramic resonator is accurate only to a few tenths of a percent, so the real period is known to about a millisecond. Cycle-exactness and clock accuracy are different things."

### L4. Line 9: "moving the program by four cells meant finding and recalculating every address inside it". Category C (sibling consistency). Severity: likely error
**Problem.** §01.05's own answer to Experiment 01.05-4 (siblings/01.05.md line 755) stresses that the data addresses (24, 25, 26) did *not* change. Only the two program addresses did.
**Fix.** "…meant finding and recalculating every address that pointed into the moved code."

### L5. Line 98: "meaning 'to here minus 2 *bytes*'" is ambiguous, and with the natural reading of "here" it is wrong. Category C. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** If "here" is the jump itself, "here − 2 bytes" is the instruction *before* the jump. In GNU as for AVR, `.±n` in a relative jump is measured from the following instruction. I checked this: linking `nop; rjmp .-2; rjmp .` gives `ff cf` (jump to itself) and `00 c0` (jump to the *next* instruction). §01.05 phrased it correctly as "2 bytes before the *next* instruction".
**Fix.** "…writes it as `rjmp .-2`, meaning '2 *bytes* back from the following instruction': the same jump, measured in bytes instead of words."

### L6. Lines 32, 596 (Self-check 1): "Each line of assembly language becomes exactly one machine instruction" and "It does not decide anything about the program". Category C. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** Directive lines, label-only lines and comments produce no instruction, as the section itself says at lines 236 and 305. Assemblers also do decide some things: GNU as for x86 chooses between short and near jump encodings and between equivalent opcodes (see E3). Many assemblers also provide macros and pseudo-instructions.
**Fix.** "Each *instruction* line becomes exactly one machine instruction, chosen by the programmer. The assembler may choose among equivalent encodings, but never what the program does." Apply the same change to Self-check 1.

### L7. Line 349: "flash address 0, where the processor begins after reset". Category C (sibling consistency). Severity: likely error
**Problem.** §01.05 (siblings/01.05.md line 249) states precisely that on an Uno a fuse makes the chip start in the bootloader, which then jumps to address 0.
**Fix.** "…sits at flash address 0, where execution of a program begins (on an Uno, after the bootloader hands over, §01.05)."

### L8. Line 455 (Chapter 01 in one view, §01.04): "feedback makes gates remember, giving flip-flops, registers and memory, volatile and non-volatile". Category C. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** Feedback explains latches, flip-flops and SRAM. It does not explain DRAM (a capacitor's charge) or flash and EEPROM (trapped charge on a floating gate). Figure 01.06-3 itself says "trapped charge on floating gates (§01.04)".
**Fix.** "**§01.04**: feedback makes gates remember, giving flip-flops, registers and SRAM; stored charge gives DRAM, and trapped charge gives non-volatile flash and EEPROM."

### L9. Line 632 (Experiment 01.06-3 answer): "eleven lines in all". Category B. Severity: likely error
**Problem.** Listing 01.05-3 as printed traces its first 9 steps (`if (steps < 9)`). A reader who pastes the bytes into it sees 9 trace lines plus 11 `OUT:` lines and the "halted" line. The author's `t3.c` had the trace switched off (`if (0)`), which the answer does not say. I ran the unmodified tiny.c with the new bytes: 9 trace lines, then OUT 10, 19, …, 55, 55, then "halted after 92 instructions".
**Fix.** "…eleven `OUT:` lines in all (after the 9 trace lines that Listing 01.05-3 prints at the start)…"

### L10. Line 29 (history): "David Wheeler, the first research student to program EDSAC". Category C. Severity: likely error (cannot be verified)
**Evidence.** The sources I found describe Initial Orders 1 as "devised by David Wheeler, then a research student, in 1949" (M. Smotherman, Clemson EDSAC page). Wikipedia says Wheeler "was a research student at the University Mathematical Laboratory … 1948–1951" and "earned the world's first Computer Science PhD". I found nothing supporting "the first research student to program EDSAC".
**Fix.** "David Wheeler, then a research student (he later received the world's first PhD in computer science)…"

### L11. Line 29 (history): Kathleen Booth. Category C. Severity: improvement / precision
**Evidence.** The 1947 work is the report *Coding for A.R.C.* by A. D. Booth and K. H. V. Britten (her name before her 1950 marriage), written at the Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton. Wikipedia lists the reference "Booth A.D. and Britten K.H.V. (1947) Coding for A.R.C., Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton" and says she "co-wrote the first assembly language". ARC stands for Automatic Relay *Calculator*.
**Fix.** "Kathleen Booth (then Kathleen Britten), working with Andrew Booth on the ARC (Automatic Relay Calculator) of Birkbeck College, London, set out a symbolic notation for programming it in their 1947 report *Coding for A.R.C.*, written during a visit to Princeton; she is generally credited with creating the first assembly language." Update the Sources line (649) to cite the report.

### Technical precision (C)
- **C1, line 11.** "The AVR has 131 instructions": the count is the ATmega328P's (other AVR cores differ).
