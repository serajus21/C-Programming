## 1. Errors

### E1. Line 480, category D, severity medium. A C `int` addition is not one instruction on the AVR
**Problem.** "C does not hide the machine's natural sizes: it *adopts* them, which is how a C `int` addition can be a single machine instruction on each machine."
**Evidence.** On the ATmega328P a 16-bit `int` addition takes two instructions. The section itself says so on line 342 ("`add`, `adc`: a 16-bit addition done as two 8-bit ones"). I checked it with `int add(int a,int b){return a+b;}` and `avr-gcc -mmcu=atmega328p -Os -c`. The result was `add r24, r22` / `adc r25, r23` / `ret`. The AVR's natural data width is 8 bits. C cannot adopt it, because the standard requires `int` to be at least 16 bits (INT_MAX >= 32767). The TOC itself calls the AVR "an 8-bit chip with 16-bit `int`". The sentence is true for x86-64 and the ESP32 but false for one of the three machines, and the text contradicts itself.
**Fix.** "C does not hide the machine's natural sizes: as far as the standard allows, it *adopts* them. On your laptop and on the ESP32 a C `int` addition is a single machine instruction. On the 8-bit AVR, `int` cannot be 8 bits, because C requires at least 16, so an `int` addition takes two (`add`, `adc`), as you saw in Listing 02.01-4. §05.03 studies these choices, and §02.02 the philosophy behind them."

### E2. Lines 563 and 670, category B, severity medium. Experiment 02.01-3 asks the reader to explain something the modified program does not show
**Problem.** "Then explain, from the addresses printed, why the AVR placed an `int` at an odd address while the laptop did not." The answer (line 670) assumes the odd address appears again.
**Evidence.** I added `short h[2]` and `long long q[2]` to steps.c and to both print lines, as the experiment asks. On simavr the program then prints `i[0] is at byte address 0x8f4, i[1] at 0x8f6`, which is **even**. I tried five declaration orders (c h i l q d; c i h l q d; c i l q d h; h c i l d q; c h i l d q), and every one gave 0x8f4. When I also printed every array's address, all six arrays were at even addresses (c 8f4, h 8ec, i 8f0, l 8e4, q 8cc, d 8dc). The odd 0x8f5 comes from the particular frame layout of the unmodified program. A reader who does the experiment will not see what the question presupposes.
**Fix (line 563).** "...Predict the results on both machines before running them. Then look at the addresses. On the laptop, every `int` address is a multiple of 4. On the AVR, the original program put `i[0]` at the odd address 0x8f5; after your change it may well be even. Explain why the AVR compiler is free to use either, while the laptop's compiler is not."
**Fix (line 670).** "...The AVR reads data one byte at a time and imposes no alignment, so the compiler may place an `int` at any address, odd or even, wherever its frame layout puts it (after the change the author's AVR build put `i[0]` at 0x8f4). x86-64 GCC aligns a 4-byte `int` to an address divisible by 4 (§06.04)."

### E3. Line 448, category A, severity low. Wrong chapter for the UART
**Problem.** "...through the chip's serial port (the UART, Chapter 36)".
**Evidence.** In the TOC, Chapter 36 ("Bare-Metal C on the ATmega328P", §36.01–§36.06) has no UART section. The UART is §37.04 "The UART at Register Level", and `printf` through the UART is §31.06 "printf on a Microcontroller" ("Output through the UART. avr-libc stdio, and retargeting."). The promises ledger has the same wrong target ("Chapter 36: the UART").
**Fix.** "(the UART, §37.04; §31.06 shows how `printf` is connected to it)". Update the promises line to "§02.01 -> §31.06, §37.04: the UART and retargeting printf".

### E4. Lines 684 and 135, category C, severity low. Ritchie does give a year for the `=+` repair
**Problem.** Line 684: "Ritchie gives no year for the reversal of `=+`; the year in the History box is a conservative 'mid-1970s'."
**Evidence.** Ritchie, *The Development of the C Language*, says the operator was spelled `=+` in B and early C, and that "this mistake, repaired in 1976," came from an easy lexer shortcut (Nokia Bell Labs copy of chist.html, checked by WebFetch).
**Fix.** Line 135: "...and C reversed it to `+=`, `-=` and the others in 1976." Line 684: replace the sentence with "Ritchie dates the repair of `=+` to 1976."

## 2. Likely errors

### L1. Line 83, category C, severity low. Date and people of the BCPL port at Bell Labs
**Problem.** "Rudd Canaday and others transported Richards's compiler to the Multics computer and to the GE-635 at Bell Labs in 1969... Among the people who used it were Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie."
**Evidence.** Ritchie's chist only cites a Canaday memo dated 1969 ([Canaday 69]). That is the date of the reference, not of the port. The Computer History Museum's BCPL collection (softwarepreservation.computerhistory.org/BCPL) quotes Ritchie: after helping port BCPL to GECOS, "During the remainder of 1968 and into 1969 I helped port the BCPL compiler to Multics". Tom Van Vleck: "Dennis Ritchie and Rudd Canaday of BTL ported CTSS BCPL to Multics." So the work spanned 1968–69, and Ritchie was one of the porters, not only a user.
**Fix.** "Rudd Canaday, Dennis Ritchie and others carried Richards's compiler to the GE-635 at Bell Labs and to the Multics computer in 1968–69, and it became the language of choice of the group that would later build Unix, Ken Thompson among them."

### L2. Lines 250 and 525, and the lower heading of Figure 02.01-2, category D, severity medium. "Byte-addressed" is unqualified for the AVR and the ESP32
**Problem.** "The PDP-11 was **byte-addressed**, ... as your ATmega328P, your laptop and your ESP32 all are." Figure 02.01-2's heading also says "Byte-addressed (PDP-11, AVR, x86-64)".
**Evidence.** §01.05 (line 379) taught the reader that "the AVR's program memory is a memory of 16-bit words, its program counter counts **words, not bytes**". §01.06 (line 590) taught that the ESP32's instruction RAM "may be read only as aligned 32-bit words". Read literally, the new sentence contradicts what this reader has just learned, and this is the section whose whole argument rests on the word/byte distinction.
**Fix.** "...as the *data* memory of your ATmega328P, your laptop and your ESP32 all is. (The AVR's flash, as §01.05 showed, is counted in 16-bit words. That is one reason a C pointer to data on the AVR holds a byte address, while the address of a function holds a word address.)" In the Terms entry, add "(for data, on every machine in this book)". In the figure heading, write "Byte-addressed data memory (PDP-11, AVR, x86-64)".

### L3. Figure 02.01-3, category E, severity medium. One front end appears to feed both pcc and GCC back ends
**Problem.** The figure draws a single "front end: reads and checks C ... written once" box with arrows to the three pcc back ends *and* the three GCC back ends. A learner will read this as pcc's and GCC's back ends sharing one front-end program. They are entirely separate compilers that share only the design. (The caption says "simplified", but the simplification here creates a false fact.) Separately, the BSTJ 1978 portability paper documents the early pcc targets as the Honeywell 6000, IBM 370 and Interdata 8/32: the Interdata compiler's code was "80 percent ... shared with the Honeywell, IBM, and other compilers". The standard PDP-11 compiler was Ritchie's own.
**Fix.** Draw two rows, each with its own front end: "pcc front end (late 1970s)" leading to Honeywell 6000, IBM 370, Interdata 8/32 and VAX-11/780; and "GCC front end (today)" leading to x86-64, AVR and Xtensa. Alternatively, keep one box but label it "front end (one per compiler: pcc's in the 1970s, GCC's today)". The note box mixes compiler parts and operating-system parts under "rewritten for each machine". Retitle it "what had to change when Unix moved" or split it in two.

### L4. Line 128, category D, severity low-medium. `const` is not a type, and was not part of the road to C
**Problem.** "The difference is the words `int`, `const` and `char *`. They are **types**... Everything else in this section explains why those few words had to be added."
**Evidence.** `const` is a type *qualifier* (C23 6.7.4), not a type. It entered C only with the 1989 standard, borrowed from C++, so nothing in this section explains why it was added. A reader who wants strict correctness will take away a wrong classification.
**Fix.** "The difference is the words `int` and `char *`. They are **types**: statements to the compiler about what each value *is*... (`const` is a later addition, from the 1989 standard, a promise that the function will not change the characters; §-ref where qualifiers are taught.) Everything else in this section explains why `int` and `char` had to be added..." The simpler alternative is to drop `const` from Listing 02.01-2, so the comparison is exactly the 1972 one.

### L5. Line 412 (Listing 02.01-5), category B/D, severity low. "115,200 baud" is only approximately what UBRR0 = 8 gives
**Problem.** `UBRR0 = 8;  // 115,200 baud at 16 MHz`
**Evidence.** In normal-speed mode the baud rate is 16 MHz / (16 × (8 + 1)) = 111,111 baud, which is 3.5% low. The ATmega328P datasheet's baud-rate table lists the same −3.5% error for this setting, beyond the roughly 2% that the datasheet's receiver-tolerance discussion recommends. Arduino's own core uses double speed for this rate: U2X0 = 1 with UBRR0 = 16, which gives 117,647 baud (+2.1%). The section only runs the program in simavr, which ignores baud error. But a reader who later flashes steps.elf to the real Uno and opens a terminal at 115,200 may see garbage and blame the code. I checked that the U2X version (`UCSR0A = (1 << U2X0); UBRR0 = 16;`) prints the same output on simavr.
**Fix.** Use `UCSR0A = (1 << U2X0); UBRR0 = 16;  // about 115,200 baud at 16 MHz (double-speed mode, as Arduino does)`. Or keep the code and change the comment to "about 111,000 baud: close enough for the simulator; on a real board see §37.04". Line numbers in the bullets are unaffected if the change stays on one line.

### L6. Lines 39 and 522, category C, severity low. BNF did not first appear for ALGOL 60, and ALGOL 60 did not introduce recursion
**Problem.** Terms: "BNF ... first used for ALGOL 60". Line 39: ALGOL 60 "introduced ... procedures that may call themselves (recursion)".
**Evidence.** Backus introduced the notation in 1959, to define the proposed International Algebraic Language (ALGOL 58), in "The Syntax and Semantics of the Proposed International Algebraic Language of the Zurich ACM-GAMM Conference" (ICIP, Paris, 1959; copy at softwarepreservation.org). Naur adapted it for the ALGOL 60 report, which made it famous. Recursion was already present in LISP (McCarthy, 1958–60). ALGOL 60 was the first widely used *algebraic* language to allow it, and the first to combine it with block structure.
**Fix.** Line 39: "It made standard ideas that every later language, C included, takes for granted: blocks..., procedures that may call themselves (recursion, which LISP also had), and a precise grammar written in a notation now called BNF (Backus–Naur Form)." Terms: "...a precise notation for a language's grammar, introduced by Backus in 1959 and made famous by the ALGOL 60 report."

## 3. Improvements

- **I1. Line 29 / Figure 02.01-1 (A/E, low).** The text says FORTRAN and COBOL "stand to its sides". The figure puts FORTRAN at the top of the central column, with an "influence" arrow into ALGOL 60. Either move FORTRAN beside COBOL, or reword: "Above it stands FORTRAN, which influenced ALGOL; beside it, COBOL and Multics..."
- **I2. Figure 02.01-2 (E, low).** The lower panel's heading includes x86-64, but its int box says "a 16-bit int ... two bytes further", which is wrong for x86-64 (4 bytes). Label the box "a 16-bit int (PDP-11, AVR)". Neither panel shows the string terminator (word 16 / `004` in the model; `'\0'` at byte 112), though the text discusses word 16. Add it, or say in the caption that the terminator is omitted.
- **I3. Line 144 (C, low).** The body states as fact that "On the PDP-7, B packed strings two characters to a word in this way". The Sources line rightly calls this secondary. The TUHS message (Lowenstein, Oct 2004) says characters "seem to have been packed as 9-bit half-words in a big-endian fashion", and it is about Unix's PDP-7 text, not B. Neither Ritchie's chist nor Thompson's manual mentions PDP-7 packing. Hedge it in the body: "(On the PDP-7, characters appear to have been packed two to a word in this way...)".
- **I4. Line 92 (C, low).** "the BCPL compiler of the time could not fit in so small a machine" is inference. Ritchie says Thompson first made "a rapidly scuttled attempt at Fortran" and then created B. He describes B as "BCPL squeezed into 8K bytes of memory" and says the B compiler's storage limits forced a one-pass design. Suggested wording: "After a brief attempt at FORTRAN, Thompson took BCPL as his model, but cut it down to fit the PDP-7's 8K words..."
- **I5. Line 368 (C, low).** (a) "gave up, because structures did not yet exist". Ritchie says only that the 1972 attempt was "before structures" and that Thompson "gave up the effort". Write "...in 1972, before C had structures, and gave up." (b) The one-third size increase was not like for like. The 1974 CACM paper adds that the C version "includes many functional improvements, including multiprogramming and the ability to share reentrant code". Mention this, since it changes how the reader should judge the cost of C.
- **I6. Line 343 (D, low).** "the byte address of word n / 2 is 2 × (n / 2)" should read "the *byte offset* of word n / 2 from s is 2 × (n / 2)". The address is s + 2 × (n / 2), and the `add`/`adc` that follow add s.
- **I7. Line 346 (D, low).** §01.06 explained relocations as necessary because "a label used in one file may be defined in another". Here the jump target is in the same function, so the reader may wonder why it is unresolved. Add: "the AVR assembler leaves even a jump within the same file for the linker to fill in". I checked that `avr-objdump -dr` shows `R_AVR_13_PCREL .text+0x18`, as the text says.
- **I8. Line 371 (D, low).** In a "Precisely speaking" box, the definition of byte should be the standard's: "an addressable unit of data storage large enough to hold any member of the basic character set", with `sizeof(char)` equal to 1 by definition. Also, "precisely so that the same language could serve word-addressed machines" states a design motive without a source. Write "and this generality is what lets the same language serve...".
- **I9. Line 135 (C, low).** Ritchie says the `=+` "notation came from Algol 68 via McIlroy". ALGOL 68 itself wrote `+:=`, so what came from it is the *idea* of combined assignment operators. Suggested: "borrowing the idea (ALGOL 68 wrote `+:=`) by way of Douglas McIlroy".
- **I10. Line 501 (C, low).** cFS was open-sourced in stages. The NASA release of March 26, 2015 announced the *application suite* and says the cFE and OSAL had already been released as open source (a code.nasa.gov cFE project page is archived from February 2013). Suggested: "...and released it as open source in stages, completing the release in 2015". The "Class B" claim was not independently verified here; the Sources line cites McComas et al.
- **I11. Line 366 (C/D, low).** "In B and BCPL, declaring an array of ten words, `auto v[10];`": that is B syntax only. BCPL wrote `let v = vec 10`. Suggest "In B (and similarly in BCPL)". Note also that the B manuals differ on the count. Thompson's PDP-11 manual gives subscripts 0 to 9, but Kernighan's Honeywell tutorial says `auto v[10]` "allocates 11 consecutive words". A reader doing Experiment 02.01-5 may notice this, so a footnote would help.
- **I12. Line 565 (A, low).** Listing 02.01-6 is never mentioned by number in the text (only in Sources). Write "Compile Listing 02.01-6 for both machines...".
- **I13. promises.md (A, low).** Besides E3, the ledger lacks Chapter 08 (character codes, line 242) and Chapter 39 / §39.06 (designing a processor, line 515). The §27.02 address-randomization promise is already flagged there as missing from the TOC bullets. Keep the flag.
- **I14. Line 83 (C, low).** TRIPOS was developed at Cambridge in 1976–78 (Richards et al., Software: Practice and Experience 9(7), 1979) and was the basis of AmigaDOS. "used ... in the early 1980s" is not wrong but is narrow. Suggest "from the late 1970s".
- **I15. Lines 252 and 255 (D, spelling).** "fitted": the usual American form is "fit" ("it fit badly", "fit in one of those machines' words").
